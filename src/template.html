Finding 01 · the building line

The county was built outward from Washington, and then it stopped

Every parcel has a distance from the Zero Milestone in Washington. Take the median of that distance for each decade's new buildings and you get the position of the building line, decade by decade — a front advancing into farmland.

How to read it: each dot is one decade. Higher means that decade's construction happened further out from the city.

How much went up

Buildings completed per decade. The two curves are the same county: one is where it built, the other is how much.

The 2020s bar is a partial decade — the file was assembled in 2025.

Put simply: in the 1920s a new house was typically about 7 miles from downtown Washington. By the 1990s it was 18 miles out. Since then that number has not moved — the county ran out of the kind of land it had been building on.
Finding 02 · the wall

In 1980 the county drew a line around a third of itself

The Agricultural Reserve — about 93,000 acres downzoned in 1980, now the AR zone — was an explicit decision to stop building in the upper county. The tax roll records what happened next.

New buildings inside the Reserve, by decade

Counted against the AR zone as mapped today. The 1980s were the county's biggest building decade anywhere else.

Inside the Reserve Rest of the county, same scale ×40

What the numbers say

Caveat worth keeping: the boundary used here is the AR zone as mapped today, applied backwards to buildings that went up before it existed. That is the honest way to ask "what is inside the line now", and the wrong way to ask "what was legal then" — the 1980 RDT boundary was redrawn more than once on its way to today's AR.

The county decided in 1980 that a large piece of its farmland would not become subdivisions. Turn on the "Agricultural Reserve" layer on the map and run the time-lapse: the upper-left third of the county stays dark while everything else fills in.
Finding 03 · what got built

The detached house stopped being the default

The assessor records a structure type for each building. Grouped into forms, they show a housing stock changing shape underneath a county that kept calling itself suburban.

Share of each decade's new buildings, by form

Detached houses, townhouses, condominium units, apartment buildings and everything else, as a share of what was completed that decade.

One caution the data forces: a unit is counted as a condominium if that is how it is owned today, not how it was built. Garden apartments converted in the 1970s still carry their 1960s construction year, which is most of the 1960s condominium block.

The median new detached house, in square feet

Recorded structure area of detached houses completed each decade.

The dip is real and it is the postwar starter house. The rise after it is not a correction — it keeps going.

The second source

2,829 of these buildings have been written about

The Maryland Historical Trust keeps an inventory of surveyed historic properties. Each entry carries a scanned survey form — typed, often decades ago, by someone who stood in front of the building. Where one lands on a parcel in the tax roll, this page joins them.

Joined by position: an inventory record is attached to the nearest tax parcel within 60 m, one parcel each. The rest are districts, parks, canal segments and archaeological sites that do not sit on a single lot — they are on the map as inventory markers, but they have no parcel to join to.
